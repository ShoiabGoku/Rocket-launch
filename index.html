    Method

    How the bench works

    One test article

    Every method carries the same payload with the same acceleration limit to the same destination. Beyond low orbit, a storable departure stage (Isp 320 s) is added to the payload stack for every ground launcher, so they all deliver identical stacks to a parking orbit. The tether and elevator release the payload on their own orbits and size their own kick stage.

    Flown, not estimated

    Rockets fly a 2-D point-mass trajectory over a rotating spherical Earth: inverse-square gravity, the US Standard Atmosphere 1976 (tables to 1,000 km), Mach-dependent drag, Isp that rises with altitude, throttling to respect the payload's g-limit, a pitch kick and gravity turn, then closed-loop guidance into a transfer orbit. Stage masses come from the rocket equation with structure fractions that worsen as vehicles shrink. The vehicle is resized against the losses it actually suffers until the flight closes.

    Gun, track and sling projectiles leave the muzzle and coast through the air with a slender-body drag curve. They pull up to tens of g of drag deceleration and take Sutton–Graves stagnation heating (q̇ = 1.74×10⁻⁴·√(ρ/rₙ)·v³). An ablator is sized from the drag work. A hardened onboard stage then lights near apoapsis. The exit angle and speed are searched for the lightest projectile that reaches orbit.

    Energy

    Energy efficiency is the payload's gain in specific orbital energy divided by everything spent: propellant combustion energy, grid electricity at the launcher, carrier-aircraft fuel, solar energy for tether reboost, and the energy to make the propellants (air separation, refining, electrolysis and liquefaction).

    Cost and readiness

    Hardware follows a TRANSCOST-style law (cost ∝ dry mass^0.6, calibrated so a Falcon 9 first stage costs about $30M). Infrastructure is spread over the campaign: more facilities are built if the launch rate exceeds one site's capacity. Costs for systems that do not exist (the maglev tube, rotovator and elevator) use published estimates and are uncertain by large factors. Readiness is NASA's TRL scale, assessed for 2026.

    Limits of the model

    • Planar flight: the launch azimuth is due east, so inclination equals latitude and GEO missions pay for the plane change.
    • Point masses: no aerodynamic lift. That penalises air launch, where Pegasus uses a wing.
    • Gun-launch heating uses a fixed heat fraction of the drag work; real ablation depends on nose shape and transition.
    • The rotovator catch and the elevator ribbon need technology that has never been demonstrated. Their numbers show what physics allows if the engineering works.
